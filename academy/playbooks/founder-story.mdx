Use a founder-story page when the origin of the product helps a customer understand **why the brand exists, what problem it set out to solve, and why its approach is credible**.

The founder is a storytelling device, not automatically the protagonist. The customer’s problem should remain the center of the page.

## When to use it

Useful when:

- the founder has a real personal / professional connection to the problem;
- the origin story explains a meaningful product decision;
- the brand is founder-led and trust / authenticity matters to the campaign;
- a creator / paid-social campaign already introduces the founder;
- the product category benefits from explaining *why this was built differently*.

Skip the founder angle when the story is mostly generic entrepreneurship and does not help the customer make a decision.

## Phase 1 — Gather the real story

Collect only source material you are willing to publish:

- founder interview / written story;
- real timeline / company origin;
- approved founder photo / video;
- product-development decisions;
- founder credentials when relevant and accurate;
- approved claims;
- customer proof.

Do not ask AI to “make the founder story emotional.” It may invent struggle, motivations, credentials, or turning points if you have not supplied them.

## Phase 2 — Find the customer-relevant spine

A useful founder story often follows:

**Customer problem → founder encounter / observation → why existing options felt insufficient → product principle → what was built → proof → customer decision**

Write the story in one sentence:

> [FOUNDER] saw / experienced **[REAL PROBLEM]**, believed **[APPROVED INSIGHT]**, and built **[PRODUCT / APPROACH]** around **[REAL PRODUCT PRINCIPLE]**.

If that sentence is weak, gather better source material before designing.

## Phase 3 — Build the page

```text
Build a founder-story landing page for [PRODUCT].

AUDIENCE
[AUDIENCE]

SOURCE MATERIAL
Use only the attached founder story, approved product facts,
AI Knowledge, and supplied customer proof.

STORY ARC
1. Start with the customer's problem / tension
2. Introduce the founder only where the story becomes relevant
3. Explain the real insight or dissatisfaction that led to the product
4. Show the product principle / what was intentionally built differently
5. Bring the story back to the customer's use case
6. Use approved proof
7. Product / purchase section
8. FAQ / founder closing where useful

Do not invent founder hardship, credentials, dates, quotes, press,
customer demand, testing stories, or motivations.
Keep the customer—not the founder—as the main beneficiary of the story.
```

## Phase 4 — Keep the founder section concrete

Strong founder copy includes specifics that explain the product:

- “I wanted a format I could actually use while traveling” — if true;
- “We chose X because…” — if supported;
- “Customers kept asking for…” — only with real evidence;
- “My background in…” — only when accurate and relevant.

Weak founder copy is generic:

> “I always dreamed of changing the industry and creating something better.”

Specific product decisions build more trust than inspirational filler.

## Phase 5 — Use the founder to explain differentiation

Ask what the founder story makes easier to believe:

- ingredient / material choice;
- format / usability decision;
- quality standard;
- routine / use case;
- business philosophy relevant to purchase;
- why a feature exists.

Do not use the founder as a substitute for evidence. “I believe in it” is not the same as a substantiated product claim.

## Phase 6 — Transition back to the customer

After the origin, shift from **“why we built it”** to **“what this means for you.”**

Useful bridge:

> “That principle led to **PRODUCT FACT**, which matters when **CUSTOMER USE CASE**.”

Then use customer proof and purchase choices to move from brand story to customer decision.

## Phase 7 — Decide founder presence on mobile

Founder pages often become image-heavy. On mobile:

- keep the opening customer-relevant;
- avoid a giant founder portrait pushing the value proposition below the fold;
- use short quotes / captions where appropriate;
- keep product imagery present before the page becomes entirely biography;
- maintain a clear purchase path.

## Phase 8 — QA credibility

Review every founder-specific assertion:

- Is the quote real?
- Is the credential accurate?
- Did this event actually happen?
- Is the timeline approved?
- Is “we created this because customers asked” actually documented?
- Does the story imply medical / scientific authority the founder does not have?

Then verify product, offer, cart, and campaign link normally.

## What good looks like

The reader should leave with:

> “I understand why this brand / product exists, the origin helps the product make more sense, and the story connects back to my problem.”

If the reader only learns that the founder is passionate, the page is not doing enough product work.

## Common mistakes

### Founder as hero, customer as audience

The page becomes a biography. Reframe around what the story means for the customer.

### Invented dramatic origin

Never fill gaps with adversity, epiphanies, or customer demand that were not supplied.

### Credentials used as decoration

Only feature credentials that materially support the product / category story.

### Product appears too late

The reader should know what type of product this story relates to even if the detailed purchase pitch comes later.

### No proof beyond the founder

Use real product facts and customer evidence to support the story.

## Useful tests

- founder-first vs customer-problem-first hero;
- short founder proof section vs full narrative;
- founder video vs text / imagery;
- product-development story vs customer-outcome story;
- founder page vs direct product page for the same audience.
