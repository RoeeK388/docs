Use this playbook when the customer needs **more context before the product pitch**—for example, when the problem is unfamiliar, the product mechanism needs explanation, or the campaign starts from a strong editorial / curiosity angle.

An advertorial should feel useful enough to keep reading, while still being honest about the brand and clear about the eventual product decision.

## When to use an advertorial

Advertorials can work well when:

- cold traffic needs education before a product page makes sense;
- the campaign angle begins with a problem, misconception, routine, or discovery;
- the product has a mechanism / approach worth explaining;
- a founder / expert / customer story genuinely helps explain the problem;
- you want to test a long-form narrative against a direct-response page.

Do not use an advertorial to disguise an ad as independent journalism or to invent publications, experts, quotes, studies, or editorial endorsements.

## The core story arc

A strong advertorial usually earns the transition in this order:

**Hook → relevance → problem → insight → solution criteria → product → proof → purchase**

The exact section count can vary. The important part is that the product appears because the story has created a reason for it, not because the page suddenly switches from article to sales page.

## Phase 1 — Define the reader question

Write one question the reader wants answered.

Examples:

- “Why does my morning routine keep getting harder to stick to?”
- “What should I actually look for in a daily hydration product?”
- “Why do so many [category] products feel the same?”

This question creates the editorial spine.

Then define the product transition:

> After understanding **[INSIGHT]**, the reader should see why **[PRODUCT / APPROACH]** is relevant.

## Phase 2 — Choose your credible source material

Gather:

- approved product facts;
- founder story, if relevant;
- expert / professional quotes only when real and approved;
- customer reviews / UGC;
- product / category education;
- brand imagery;
- campaign angle / ad.

Separate **source** from **style**. A reference advertorial can show you pacing and layout; it is not a source for your brand’s claims.

## Phase 3 — Build the editorial outline first

Use:

```text
Do not build the page yet.
Create an advertorial outline for [PRODUCT] aimed at [AUDIENCE].

Reader question:
[QUESTION]

Campaign angle:
[ANGLE]

Approved product facts / sources:
[ATTACH / LIST]

The article should:
- hook around the reader's problem or curiosity;
- make the problem feel specific and relevant;
- explain the useful insight / mechanism using only approved facts;
- establish the criteria a good solution should meet;
- introduce [PRODUCT] naturally against those criteria;
- use approved proof;
- transition clearly to the purchase decision.

Do not invent editorial publications, experts, research, statistics, reviews, customer results, or urgency.
Return only the outline and purpose of each section.
```

Review the outline. If the product appears before the reader has enough context, fix the story before styling.

## Phase 4 — Build the page

```text
Build the advertorial from the approved outline.

STYLE
Editorial, credible, easy to scan, not a fake newspaper.
Use clear section headings, readable body copy, and purposeful imagery.

SOURCE RULES
- Use AI Knowledge and attached source material for factual claims.
- Use supplied quotes only.
- Connected product = real product / purchase information.

TRANSITION
When the product is introduced, make the connection explicit:
“Because [INSIGHT / CRITERIA], [PRODUCT] was designed / is relevant because [APPROVED PRODUCT FACT].”

PURCHASE
Keep the product / purchase section clearly brand-owned and functional.
Do not disguise the CTA as an editorial link.
```

## Phase 5 — Tighten the opening

The first 20% of the page earns the rest of the read.

Check:

- headline creates a specific reason to continue;
- opening speaks to the audience, not the brand;
- the product is not over-sold before the problem is established;
- paragraphs are short enough for mobile;
- the page makes clear who is speaking when a founder / expert is involved.

Use:

```text
Rewrite only the opening 3 sections for faster mobile reading.
Keep all approved facts and the editorial angle.

Make the first paragraph immediately relevant to [AUDIENCE].
Remove repeated setup and generic category language.
Do not mention the product earlier unless it is necessary for transparency / clarity.
```

## Phase 6 — Make the insight useful

The middle of an advertorial should teach something. If it only repeats “the problem is bad,” the reader has no reason to believe the product is different.

Ask:

- Is there a real mechanism / criterion / framework?
- Can the reader use this insight even before buying?
- Is the insight supported by approved source material?
- Does it naturally create a reason the product fits?

Avoid pseudo-science or over-explaining technical details just to sound credible.

## Phase 7 — Introduce the product without whiplash

Weak transition:

> “That’s why thousands of people love PRODUCT. Buy now.”

Stronger transition:

> The story established **what a solution needs to do**, then the product is introduced as one implementation of those criteria using actual product facts.

Use product imagery and proof after the transition so the page begins to feel more like a purchase experience at the right moment.

## Phase 8 — Add social proof where it answers objections

Do not dump every review into one section.

Map proof to questions:

- Does it fit the routine?
- Does it taste / feel / work as expected?
- Is it easy to use?
- Why did someone switch?
- What purchase concern did a customer have?

Use approved reviews and preserve attribution.

## Phase 9 — Make the purchase section feel expected

By the time the Buy Box / purchase section appears, the reader should understand:

- what the product is;
- why it relates to the problem;
- the key approved differentiation;
- enough proof to consider buying;
- what happens when they click.

The purchase section should not introduce an entirely new offer or product story.

## Phase 10 — QA credibility

Before launch, review for:

- fake publication / masthead cues;
- unsupported “experts say” wording;
- statistics without approved source;
- reviews that are paraphrased into stronger claims;
- medical / scientific language beyond approved claims;
- fake bylines / editorial independence;
- product pricing / offer mismatch;
- overly hidden brand ownership.

Then QA the live purchase, tracking, and campaign URL.

## What good looks like

The reader should be able to say:

> “I learned **X**, now I understand why this product is relevant, and the transition to buying it makes sense.”

If the reader would feel tricked when the product appears, the page is too disguised. If the product appears before anything useful is learned, the page is just a long PDP with article styling.

## Common mistakes

### Fake editorial authority

Do not invent publication names, independent ratings, expert bylines, or “breaking news” framing.

### Product appears in sentence two

If the campaign needs education, earn the transition.

### Article never gets to the decision

An advertorial is still a landing page. Make the product and next action clear when the story is ready.

### Too much copy because “advertorials are long”

Length is a result of the story, not the goal. Remove anything that does not move the reader toward understanding or decision.

### Unsupported mechanism language

Simplify to what the brand can actually substantiate.

## Useful tests

- problem-led vs curiosity-led headline;
- founder-led vs editorial narrator;
- product introduced earlier vs later;
- proof before vs after product introduction;
- full advertorial vs shorter educational page.
