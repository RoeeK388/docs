Use this playbook after an experiment has produced enough information for your team to make a decision or ask a better question.

The goal is not to manufacture a dramatic “winner” story. The goal is to capture **what the test changed, what the observed result suggests, what remains uncertain, and what you should learn next**.

## Phase 1 — Restate the original question

Before looking for a new idea, remind the team what the test was supposed to answer.

Write:

```text
HYPOTHESIS
We believed [CHANGE] would improve [BEHAVIOR / METRIC] because [REASON].

CONTROL
[WHAT CONTROL DID]

VARIANT
[WHAT VARIANT DID DIFFERENTLY]

PRIMARY METRIC
[METRIC]
```

This protects the analysis from drifting toward whichever metric looks most interesting after the fact.

## Phase 2 — Separate observation from explanation

Two levels matter:

**Observation:** what the experiment metrics actually show.

**Explanation:** why you think that happened.

Example:

- Observation: Variant B has higher observed CVR over the selected test period.
- Explanation hypothesis: The proof-led hero reduced skepticism for cold traffic.

The first is a result. The second is an interpretation that may deserve another test or supporting evidence.

Use Jurni AI to keep the distinction clean:

```text
Help me review this experiment.

Original hypothesis: [HYPOTHESIS]
Primary metric: [METRIC]
Results: [PASTE JURNI RESULTS]
Known campaign / implementation changes: [NOTES]

Separate your response into:
1. Observations directly supported by the supplied result
2. Plausible interpretations / hypotheses
3. Things the result does not tell us
4. Checks that could invalidate or complicate the interpretation

Do not invent confidence, customer intent, or causal certainty.
```

## Phase 3 — Look at supporting behavior

Supporting metrics can help explain the journey, but they should not replace the primary metric because they happen to look better.

Examples:

- higher CTR but flat CVR may mean the page encouraged more clicks without improving purchase completion;
- higher Add-to-Cart Rate but lower checkout completion may move friction later in the journey;
- higher CVR with lower AOV can still produce a different revenue outcome than CVR alone suggests;
- higher AOV based on a small number of orders can be volatile and should be interpreted in context.

Use supporting metrics to ask **where the journey changed**, not to cherry-pick a win.

## Phase 4 — Check for confounds

Before capturing the learning, ask:

- Did product / offer change during the test?
- Did one variant have a bug or cart issue?
- Did traffic weights or routing change?
- Did the campaign audience change materially?
- Did one page receive meaningful direct traffic outside the experiment?
- Did tracking or consent behavior differ?
- Did the analysis window include periods before / after the intended test?

If the result is operationally compromised, the right next action may be **rerun cleanly**, not invent a CRO insight.

## Phase 5 — Decide what you can reasonably learn

A useful learning is specific but proportional.

**Too broad:** “Customers prefer social proof.”

**Better:** “For this cold Meta campaign, moving the approved proof into the first screen was associated with stronger purchase conversion than the original hero during this test.”

The second statement preserves the context. You can later test whether the principle generalizes.

## Phase 6 — Record the decision

Use a lightweight test record:

```text
WHAT WE TESTED
[CONTROL VS VARIANT]

WHY
[HYPOTHESIS]

PRIMARY RESULT
[OBSERVED RESULT]

SUPPORTING SIGNALS
[OTHER RELEVANT METRICS / NOTES]

LEARNING
[PROPORTIONAL CONCLUSION]

LIMITATIONS
[WHAT WE STILL DO NOT KNOW]

DECISION
[ADOPT / KEEP CONTROL / KEEP RUNNING / INVESTIGATE / RERUN]
```

This record is more valuable six months later than a screenshot labeled “winner.”

## Phase 7 — Generate the next questions

Now ask what remains uncertain.

If a benefit-led hero performs better than a feature-led hero, next questions could include:

- Which benefit resonates most?
- Does the effect hold for different traffic sources?
- Was the result driven by the headline or the reduced copy density?
- Does additional proof amplify the benefit-led message?

If a variant underperforms, do not automatically test a more extreme version. Ask whether the underlying hypothesis was wrong, the execution was weak, or the page changed too many things.

## Phase 8 — Pick the next test based on learning value

Use:

```text
Use this completed experiment to recommend the next test.

Original hypothesis: [HYPOTHESIS]
Observed result: [RESULT]
What changed: [DIFFERENCE]
Supporting signals: [SIGNALS]
Known limitations: [LIMITATIONS]

Generate 4 possible next tests.
For each:
- question it answers;
- single variable to change;
- why it follows from this result;
- what stays fixed;
- primary metric;
- what we would learn from either outcome.

Rank them by learning value and practical relevance.
Do not assign fake conversion-lift estimates.
```

Prefer tests that reduce an important uncertainty rather than simply generate more variants.

## Phase 9 — Turn the learning into other marketing work

Some experiment learnings can inform more than another landing-page test.

Examples:

- a strong message angle can become new ad creative;
- a recurring objection can influence email / SMS education;
- an effective creator framing can inform creator briefs;
- an offer explanation that reduces confusion can improve other campaign pages;
- a weak purchase choice can trigger product / merchandising investigation.

Do not blindly copy a winning element to every channel. Translate the **learning**, not the exact design.

## What good looks like

At the end of the review, your team should have:

- one primary decision;
- one concise learning;
- an explicit limitation / unknown;
- one next test or action with a reason.

If you leave with ten unrelated CRO ideas, the review was not prioritized enough.

## Common mistakes

### “Winner = universal best practice”

A result is tied to the tested audience, traffic, page, offer, and period. Generalize only with further evidence.

### Explaining the result with customer psychology you never measured

Use customer-behavior explanations as hypotheses unless you have research or other evidence.

### Ignoring implementation issues because the result is exciting

A broken control or tracking discrepancy can make a strong-looking result meaningless.

### Testing the exact same idea forever

Once a question is sufficiently answered for a business decision, move to the next meaningful uncertainty.
