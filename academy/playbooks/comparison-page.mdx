# Build a product comparison page

Use a comparison page when the visitor is actively deciding between **your product and a real alternative, product types, or purchase options**.

A comparison is persuasive when it reduces decision complexity. It becomes weak when every row says you win or when claims cannot be supported.

## Decide what kind of comparison you are making

### Product versus alternative

Use when customers commonly compare you with another product or approach and you have approved, supportable evidence.

### Product type versus product type

Example: ready-to-drink versus powder, one-step versus multi-step, or another category-level decision.

### Your own options

Use when the customer needs help choosing between variants, bundles, sizes, or purchase plans.

Each version needs different evidence and a different CTA.

## Step 1: define the real decision criteria

Do not start by listing every product feature. Ask what shoppers actually compare.

```text
AUDIENCE
[audience]

ALTERNATIVES
[what they are deciding between]

TOP DECISION CRITERIA
1. [criterion]
2. [criterion]
3. [criterion]
4. [criterion]
5. [criterion]

APPROVED EVIDENCE
[source for each claim]

PRIMARY ACTION
[action]
```

A strong criterion is specific enough to matter: “serving format,” “delivery cadence,” “ingredients,” “price per approved unit,” or “setup time,” where supported. “Quality” is too vague unless you define how it is measured.

## Step 2: choose fair framing

Comparisons build trust when they are visibly fair.

Avoid:

- invented competitor weaknesses
- outdated competitor pricing
- implying an alternative lacks a feature without evidence
- “X” marks on every competitor row with no nuance
- comparing your best plan to someone else's least favorable option without explaining it

If a competitor value is not reliable, use a category-level comparison or omit the row.

## Copy-ready Jurni prompt

```text
Build a comparison landing page for [audience] deciding between [our product] and [alternative / category].

DECISION CRITERIA
Use only these approved criteria:
1. [criterion + approved values]
2. [criterion + approved values]
3. [criterion + approved values]
4. [criterion + approved values]

GOAL
Help the shopper understand the meaningful differences and decide whether [product] fits their needs.

BUILD
- campaign-matched hero
- concise explanation of who this comparison is for
- comparison section using the approved values only
- short explanation for the 2–3 criteria that need context
- approved proof
- clear product / purchase transition
- FAQ for remaining comparison questions

TONE
Confident and factual, not insulting.

DO NOT
- invent competitor values
- create superiority claims beyond the supplied evidence
- use fake ratings or badges
- imply every shopper should prefer our product

MOBILE
Convert wide comparison tables into readable stacked cards or rows without hiding criteria.
```

## Step 3: make the table a summary, not the whole argument

The table should help a shopper scan. Important criteria may still need a short explanation below it.

For example, if the table says “single-serve packets,” a supporting section can explain why that format matters to the intended audience using approved product information.

Avoid squeezing paragraphs into table cells.

## Step 4: explain who your product is best for

A credible comparison can say:

> “Best for shoppers who prioritize [approved criteria].”

This is often stronger than pretending your product is universally better.

If an alternative genuinely fits a different need, acknowledge the tradeoff. Clarity can increase trust.

## Step 5: keep prices and offers current

Pricing comparisons are especially sensitive to staleness. If you show price, savings, or subscription terms:

- confirm current approved values
- specify the comparable unit or plan
- review market/currency implications
- avoid hard-coding values that change frequently unless the team owns the update process

The purchase section should always use the real current product data.

## Step 6: make mobile comparison usable

Large tables often fail on phones. Prefer:

- stacked criterion cards
- sticky first column only when genuinely usable
- one criterion per row with clearly labeled products
- short copy

Use:

```text
Keep the desktop comparison structure.
On mobile, convert the comparison into stacked criterion cards.
Each card must show the criterion, our approved value, and the alternative value without horizontal scrolling.
Keep all comparison wording unchanged.
```

## Step 7: review every claim before launch

```text
Audit this comparison page against the supplied approved evidence.
Do not edit yet.

For every comparative statement, return:
- CLAIM
- SOURCE PROVIDED? yes/no
- CURRENT / TIME-SENSITIVE? yes/no
- NEEDS QUALIFICATION? yes/no

Flag any wording that goes beyond the supplied evidence.
Flag any number likely to become stale.
```

## What good looks like

A visitor should be able to scan the comparison and understand the **few differences that actually matter to their purchase**.

The page should feel useful even to someone who is skeptical of your brand.

## Common mistakes

**Comparing everything.** More rows often make the decision harder.

**Unverifiable competitor claims.** A weak comparison can hurt trust more than no comparison.

**One-sided criteria.** Choosing only dimensions designed to make you win looks manipulative.

**Outdated prices.** Review time-sensitive information before each campaign.

**Desktop-only table.** Comparison must remain readable on mobile.

## What to test next

- comparison table near hero versus later
- 3 high-value criteria versus a more comprehensive table
- product versus category framing
- comparison-first page versus problem/education-first page
